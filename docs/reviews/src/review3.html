Performance Evaluation, Comparison and Conclusion

This document presents the evaluation of the adaptive scheduler described in Review 2, its comparison with existing systems, and the conclusion and future work.

1. Experimental Setup

1.1 Runs

Each of the six workloads (W1 to W6, described in Review 2) was generated with ten different random seeds, giving 60 workload instances of 200 processes each. All eleven schedulers were run on every instance, for a total of 660 runs. Because every scheduler sees exactly the same processes, the results can be compared seed by seed (paired comparison) rather than as independent averages. In addition, 120 runs were used for an ablation study, 130 for parameter sweeps and 150 for testing different context-switch costs.

1.2 Metrics

For a process i with arrival time ai, completion time ci, time of first execution si, total CPU time Bi and total I/O time IOi, the following are measured [1]:

Turnaround Ti = ci − ai     Waiting Wi = Ti − Bi − IOi     Response Ri = si − ai

Along with the averages, the worst-case waiting time, the number of context switches, CPU utilisation, Jain's fairness index [2], the number of starved processes (those that waited more than five times the average wait of all schedulers on that workload) and the prediction error (MAE) are recorded. To show how close a scheduler gets to the best case, the share of the gap to the oracle that it closes is also computed:

Gap closed = (WSJF-EMA − Wproposed) / (WSJF-EMA − Woracle) × 100%

1.3 Choice of the ageing weight

The ageing weight β controls a trade-off: a larger value protects long-waiting processes but pushes the order towards FCFS. Since the plan did not fix a value, it was tuned on three separate seeds (100, 101, 102) that are not part of the 660 evaluation runs, so the results are not tuned to the test data. Each value was scored by its average and worst-case waiting time relative to SJF-EMA, and β = 0.03 gave the best overall score (Fig. 1).

The clearest result is on the mixed workload W4, which is also the most realistic one. Here the proposed scheduler waits 1,786 ms against 2,460 ms for SJF with exponential averaging, a reduction of 27.4%. It is better on all ten seeds, and the mean paired improvement (674 ms) is far larger than its standard deviation (117 ms). This closes 54% of the gap between SJF-EMA and the oracle. On W1 and W5 the average is within 2% of SJF-EMA. On the uniform short-burst workloads W2 and W3, and on the overloaded W6, it is slightly worse, by 14%, 5% and 3% respectively.

2.2 Worst-case waiting time and starvation

Average waiting time hides processes that wait for a very long time. Thanks to the ageing term, the proposed scheduler lowers the worst case by 56% on W1, 27% on W3, 23% on W4 and 53% on W5, and on five of the six workloads it is better than both SJF-EMA and SRTF-EMA. On W5 the number of starved processes per run drops from 7.7 with SJF-EMA to 0.2.

2.3 Context switches and response time

Round Robin shows the usual trade-off on W4: a 2 ms quantum gives a fast first response but 34,354 context switches, while a 20 ms quantum cuts the switches to 6,542 but raises the average response time to 69 ms. The proposed scheduler needs 5,933 switches, 61% fewer than RR with a 5 ms quantum, and has an average response time of 15 ms, so it does not have to give up responsiveness to save switches. When the cost of a context switch was raised from 0.1 ms to 0.5 ms, its average waiting time on W4 grew by 20%, compared with 53% for MLFQ and 62% for RR with a 5 ms quantum.

2.4 Prediction accuracy

The learned model has a lower prediction error than exponential averaging on all six workloads, with reductions between 12% and 22% (Fig. 3). The improvement is largest on I/O-bound processes, where exponential averaging reacts too much to individual random bursts, while the model learns to predict close to the typical burst length.

2.5 Adaptation of the time quantum

To check that the quantum really follows the workload, the scheduler was run on a workload whose mix changes every 30 seconds (Fig. 4). The quantum stays around 6 ms while interactive processes arrive, rises to the 100 ms limit for CPU-bound processes, and drops to about 3.5 ms for I/O-bound processes. This is close to the theoretical 80th percentile of their bursts (3.2 ms). No single fixed quantum suits all three phases.

Removing ageing lowers the average on W4 a little, but the worst case rises by 26% on W4 and doubles on W5, so ageing is what keeps long processes from starving. The proposed scheduler has a lower average waiting time than every classical algorithm except SRTF-EMA, which is 3% better on average. SRTF-EMA, however, has a 35% higher worst-case wait and 16% more context switches, because it preempts the running process on every arrival. MLFQ comes close on average waiting time and responds faster, since new processes always start in its top queue, but it needs twice as many context switches. Round Robin with 5 to 20 ms quanta keeps a lower worst case, as expected from its strict rotation, but the proposed scheduler has an 18% to 31% lower average waiting time than these.

The same comparison on the phase-changing workload W5 is shown in Fig. 5 for one seed. Here plain FCFS has the lowest average waiting time of all, even lower than the oracle. With processes that have many bursts, always running the shortest burst first (as SJF, SRTF and the oracle do) repeatedly delays processes in their long phase, and this dominates the average. It also shows that the oracle is a strong reference for burst ordering, but not an absolute lower bound when processes have many bursts.

The main difference from the dynamic-quantum Round Robin variants [7]–[10], and from proportional-share methods such as lottery scheduling [19], is that the proposed scheduler never assumes that burst lengths are known; it predicts them from history and corrects the quantum using its own prediction errors. Compared with earlier prediction work [11], [12], the prediction is used both for ordering and for the quantum, and an ageing term protects long processes. Unlike whole-job run-time prediction [13], [14] and heavier learning methods for clusters [15], [16] or for modelling the Linux scheduler [18], the approach here is light and works on individual CPU bursts, which addresses the overhead concern raised in [17], though it is still more costly than the fair-share scheduler in Linux [20].

4. Conclusion

This project set out to check whether predicting CPU bursts, and using the prediction both to order the ready queue and to size the time quantum, makes a measurable difference compared with the classical algorithms. The answer depends on the workload. On the mixed workload, where short interactive bursts compete with long CPU-bound ones, the proposed scheduler reduced the average waiting time by 27% compared with SJF using exponential averaging. It was better on every seed, closed more than half the gap to the oracle, cut the worst-case wait by 23%, and needed 61% fewer context switches than Round Robin with a 5 ms quantum. The ageing term roughly halved the worst-case waiting time on CPU-bound and phase-changing workloads, and the learned predictor was 12–22% more accurate than exponential averaging everywhere.

It did not help on every workload. When all processes have short random bursts (I/O-bound and interactive workloads), there is nothing to predict beyond the average, and preempting at a small quantum only adds switches, so the scheduler was 5–14% worse than SJF there. Under heavy overload, the ageing term pushes the order towards FCFS. We also found that better prediction accuracy by itself brings only a small gain; most of the benefit comes from how the prediction is used. Overall, the results support the main idea of applying the textbook quantum rule automatically from live predictions, while showing where its limits are.

5. Future Work

  • Real workloads: test the scheduler on burst traces collected from real programs instead of synthetic workloads, which would also show whether the online refit helps when the workload differs from the training data.
  • Kernel implementation: move the quantum controller into a teaching kernel such as xv6 [21] to measure its real overhead.
  • Cheaper prediction: replace the gradient boosting model, which takes about 0.5 ms per prediction in Python, with a small decision tree or lookup table that a kernel could afford.
  • Better ageing under overload: scale the ageing term with the load, so that the order does not drift towards FCFS when waits become very long.
  • Multi-core systems: extend the simulator to several CPUs, where predicted bursts could also guide load balancing.
