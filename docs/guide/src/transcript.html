1 – Title 15 s

Shivaansh

Good morning ma'am. I'm Shivaansh, and this is Devansh. Our project is a CPU scheduler that predicts how long a program will run next, and uses that to decide which program runs and for how long.

2 – Recap: problem and approach 45 s

Shivaansh

The classical algorithms are static. FCFS gives 17 ms average wait on the 24, 3, 3 example; SJF gives 3 ms but needs the next burst, which the OS doesn't know; and Round Robin's quantum is fixed by hand. The usual estimate, exponential averaging, lags when a process changes behaviour.

Devansh

So we predict the next burst with a gradient boosting model, which is 14% more accurate, and use it twice: to pick who runs, with ageing so nothing starves, and to set how long it runs using the textbook 80% rule, automatically.

3 – Experimental setup 25 s

Shivaansh

We tested 6 workload types, 11 schedulers and 10 seeds, 660 paired runs, so every scheduler sees exactly the same processes. The model and the ageing weight were tuned on separate seeds, never on the test data.

4 – The simulator matches the textbook 25 s

Devansh

Before any ML, we verified the simulator: FCFS gives exactly 17, shortest-first 3, Round Robin 5.67 and SRTF 6.5, and all 37 tests pass.

5 – Main result 30 s

Shivaansh

On the mixed workload, the realistic case, our scheduler has 27% less waiting than SJF, and it is better on all 10 seeds. It closes 54% of the gap to the oracle, which knows the future, and uses 61% fewer context switches than Round Robin with 5 ms.

6 – Live run 30 s

Devansh

Optional: run python demo.py --part 3 in Terminal; otherwise point at the screenshot.

This is a real run of all 11 schedulers on one workload. Ours, highlighted, waits about 1,900 ms against 2,800 for SJF, with a lower worst case and far fewer switches than Round Robin.

7 – Results across workloads 30 s

Shivaansh

Across all six workloads, the clear win is the mixed workload. Ageing halves the worst-case wait on CPU-bound and phase-changing work. To be honest about the limits, it is 14% worse on all-I/O work, where bursts are tiny and random.

8 – Why it works 30 s

Devansh

The quantum follows the workload: about 6 ms for interactive, close to 100 ms for CPU-bound and 3.5 ms for I/O-bound. Switching parts off shows that without ageing the worst case doubles, and that prediction alone gives only 5%. The gain comes from using the prediction twice.

9 – Comparison with existing schedulers 30 s

Shivaansh

Against existing schedulers, ours has the lowest average wait except SRTF, which is 3% better on average but has a 35% higher worst case. MLFQ is close but needs twice the switches. And unlike the papers we studied, we never assume burst lengths are known.

10 – Conclusion and future work 25 s

Shivaansh

So applying the textbook quantum rule automatically from predictions works: 27% less waiting on mixed workloads, with the worst case halved. Next, we'd test on real program traces and move it into xv6. Thank you, ma'am.

If asked – one-line answers

Why ML? (Devansh) 12–22% more accurate than exponential averaging, but the big gain comes from using the prediction twice.

Is the simulator correct? (Shivaansh) Exact textbook answers and 37 tests pass.

What if the prediction is wrong? (Devansh) The process still runs; ageing guarantees it gets the CPU, and the quantum corrects with recent errors.

Did you tune on test data? (Devansh) No, training and tuning used separate seeds.

Real OS? (Shivaansh) It's a controlled experiment; a kernel would need a smaller model, and xv6 is next.
